Shajarah

Keeps the web working when your phone loses signal.

  • Downloads what you'll read next. When the signal starts to fade, the pages you're most likely to click are fetched ahead of time, so they open instantly even with no signal at all.
  • Survives network changes. The connection runs over QUIC, which carries on when the phone's IP address changes instead of starting a new handshake the way TCP must.
  • Switches networks early. If another network is available, the connection moves there before the current one drops.

The problem

Phones ignore a fading signal until the connection actually breaks. Only then do they look for another network, reconnect, and retry whatever you were loading. On a train, in a car, or walking out of a building, that means a frozen page, a dropped call.

The two usual remedies each cover half of the problem. Modern transport protocols such as QUIC can carry a connection from one network to another, but only after the switch, and they can't load anything while there is no signal at all. Prefetching can store pages in advance, but doing it constantly wastes data on pages nobody opens.

  1. Watch the signal. The phone measures the signal on every network ten times a second and fits a trend. If the current network is heading below usable strength within the next eight seconds or so, it raises a warning.
  2. Prefetch before the drop. The phone tells the server a dropout is coming, and the server starts sending extra pages: the ones you're most likely to open next, and the likely pages after those.
  3. Open pages without signal. While there's no connection, pages you've been sent open straight from the phone's storage, as if nothing had happened.
  4. Switch networks when possible. If another network is healthy, the connection moves there before the current one fails. Because it runs on QUIC, nothing in progress is lost.

To decide which pages are worth sending, the server asks a decision model, Jev by TypeSafe, how likely you are to click each link, given the page's title, the last few pages you read and the links on the page. Only titles and link text are sent, never page content. When nothing is about to go wrong, the server sends very little.

Results

Our main test is a drive through a 45-second tunnel with no signal on any network. Twenty simulated readers each browse along their own path of real Wikipedia clicks; every setup replays exactly the same readers and clicks, so the only difference is the connection.

Wait: the total time a reader spent looking at a page that was still loading, per trip. Ordinary: TCP, which reconnects only after the link has failed, as most apps do. Modern: QUIC, which survives network changes but doesn't prefetch. 20 readers per setup.

Compared reader by reader with the modern connection, Shajarah was faster for 8 of the 20 test cases, and slower for none of them. The other readers clicked a page the model hadn't expected and waited for the signal to return, exactly as they would have without Shajarah. The typical reader waited for a page to load for only 3.4 seconds, instead of 17 seconds.

Switching networks early matters the most for anything live. On a walk out of a building's Wi-Fi, a simulated voice call (50 packets a second) that switched after Wi-Fi had died went silent for about a second and lost 2% of its audio. With Shajarah switching a few seconds before the drop, the longest gap was 60 ms and the only packet loss was the network's normal 0.4%.

What's new, and what we learned

Link prefetching and connection migration both exist. What Shajarah adds is timing: it predicts the dropout from the fading signal, and that warning tells the server when to prefetch, how much, and when to stop. The same warning also triggers an early network switch when there's somewhere to switch to. Building it taught us two things that matter for anyone combining them:

  • Never prefetch into a dying link. Our first version kept sending pages after the signal had gone. The unacknowledged data filled the connection when coverage returned, and some readers ended up waiting longer than with no prefetching at all. The server now stops just before the predicted dropout and never has more than 256 KB of packets in flight, because the predicted time can be a few seconds off.
  • QUIC waits too long after a long outage. While the link is down, QUIC doubles its retry interval with every failed attempt; after 45 seconds, the next retry was half a minute away, so the connection stayed silent long after the signal came back. We reset that cooldown as soon as the client responds again.

How we tested it

Everything ran on an emulated network on Linux. The phone, a router, our server and the websites each live in their own network namespace, and the phone's Wi-Fi, 5G and satellite links had a realistic delay, loss and bandwidth. We scheduled the link to fade and cut out over specific times to simulate real-world conditions.

We chose to test Shajarah using Wikipedia as the website that the clients are loading, as it is one of the only large public records of which links people actually click. Each reader follows links that real readers chose in August 2026, with realistic reading times, across about 2,000 Wikipedia pages frozen for the tests. We scored the click predictions separately against July's clicks. We also tested Shajarah on ordinary sites (a news front page, documentation, a shop, a forum, a government service) to make sure it isn't tuned only to Wikipedia.

Limitations

  • Prefetching costs data. About 3 MB per dropout, most of it never read. That's roughly one photo from a phone camera or a couple of minutes of music streaming, and it's spent mainly in the seconds before a dropout rather than on every page.
  • The network is emulated. The signal curves are hand-made; replaying real drive-test measurements is the next step.
  • Privacy. The prototype calls Jev through a hosted API. In a production system the model would run on our own server, so page titles and link text never leave infrastructure we control.
