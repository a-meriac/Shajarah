Browsing that sees the dropout coming

Phones switch between Wi-Fi, 5G and satellite all the time, and now and then lose signal altogether. The signal nearly always fades before it drops. Shajarah acts on that warning: it moves your connection to a better network before the old one fails, and quietly downloads the pages you're likely to open next, so a tunnel or a lift doesn't leave you staring at a spinner.

The problem

A phone treats a weakening signal as fine right up until the moment it isn't. Only once the connection has actually failed does it look for another network, reconnect, and retry whatever you were loading. On a train, in a car, or walking out of a building, that means a frozen page, a dropped call, or a spinner that lasts as long as the tunnel.

The two usual remedies each cover half of the problem. Modern transport protocols such as QUIC can carry a connection from one network to another, but only after the switch, and they have nothing to show you while there is no network at all. Prefetching can fill a cache in advance, but done all the time it burns data on pages nobody reads.

Websites
  1. Watch the signal. The phone measures the signal on every network ten times a second and fits a trend. If the current network is heading below usable strength within the next eight seconds or so, it raises a warning.
  2. Switch before it breaks. If another network is healthy, the connection moves there immediately. The tunnel runs on QUIC, which can change networks without reconnecting, so nothing in progress is lost.
  3. Stock up when there's nowhere to go. In a tunnel every network fails together. The phone tells the server a dropout is coming; the server lowers its bar for prefetching and sends the pages you're most likely to open next, and the most likely pages after those.
  4. Read through the gap. Pages you open while offline come straight from the phone's cache, as if the network were still there.

To decide which pages are worth sending, the server asks a decision model, Jev by TypeSafe, how likely you are to click each link, given the page's title, the last few pages you read and the links on the page. Only titles and link text are sent, never page content. When nothing is about to go wrong, the server sends very little.

Results

Compared reader by reader with the modern connection, Shajarah was faster for 8 of the 20 and slower for none. The remaining readers clicked a page the model hadn't anticipated and waited for the signal to return, exactly as they would have without Shajarah. The typical reader waited 3.4 seconds instead of 17.

Switching early matters most for anything live. On a walk out of a building's Wi-Fi, a simulated voice call (50 packets a second) that switched after Wi-Fi had died went silent for about a second and lost 2% of its audio. With Shajarah switching 5.8 seconds before the drop, the longest gap was 60 ms and the only loss was the network's normal 0.4%.

What's new, and what we learned

Connection migration and link prefetching both exist. What Shajarah adds is a single prediction driving both: the same fading signal that triggers an early switch also tells the server whether to prefetch, how much, and until when. Building it taught us two things that matter for anyone combining them:

  • Never prefetch into a dying link. Our first version kept sending pages after the signal had gone. The unacknowledged data jammed the connection when coverage returned, and some readers ended up waiting longer than with no prefetching at all. The server now stops just before the predicted dropout and never has more than 256 KB in flight, because the predicted time can be a few seconds off.
  • QUIC waits too long after a long outage. While the link is down, QUIC doubles its retry interval with every failed attempt; after 45 seconds, the next retry was half a minute away, so the connection stayed silent long after the signal came back. We reset that back-off as soon as the other end is heard from again.

How we tested it

Everything runs in an emulated network on Linux. The phone, a router, our server and the websites each live in their own network namespace, and the phone's Wi-Fi, 5G and satellite links are shaped to realistic delay, loss and bandwidth. Scripted scenarios fade and cut those links on a fixed timeline.

The readers come from Wikipedia's public clickstream, the only large public record of which links people actually click. Each reader follows links that real readers chose in August 2026, with realistic reading times, across about 2,000 Wikipedia pages frozen for the tests. We scored the click predictions separately against July's clicks, and checked the pipeline on ordinary sites (a news front page, documentation, a shop, a forum, a government service) to make sure it isn't tuned to Wikipedia.

Limitations

  • Click prediction is modest. Jev's three favourite links catch 16% of real clicks, against 9% for simply taking the first links on the page. When it misses, you wait as long as you would have anyway; the gains come from the hits, never at the expense of the misses.
  • Prefetching costs data. About 3 MB per tunnel, most of it never read. That is a fair trade on an unmetered connection and a poor one on a small data plan. The prefetching policy can already work to a smaller budget on metered links; detecting a metered connection automatically is still to do.
  • The network is emulated. The signal curves are hand-made; replaying real drive-test measurements is the next step.
  • Privacy. The prototype calls Jev through a hosted API. In a production system the model would run on our own server, so page titles and link text never leave infrastructure we control.
